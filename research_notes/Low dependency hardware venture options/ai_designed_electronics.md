# AI-designed small-batch electronics as a solo venture (state: November 2026)

Scope: three variants — (a) niche low-voltage products sold online, (b) fast-turnaround custom design service, (c) test fixtures / end-of-line testers.

Method note (read first): page fetching was blocked, so every figure below comes from search-result extracts, not from the full page. All 28 permitted searches were used. Where an extract did not make clear which of several pages a number came from, that is flagged. Vendor marketing and independent evidence are labelled separately. Anything I know from before this session but could not confirm by search is placed under "Gaps" and marked "unconfirmed prior knowledge".

## 1. State of AI for electronics design in 2026 (tools, benchmarks, failure types, automated checks)

### Takeaway
Independent benchmarks published in 2026 still show low end-to-end success for frontier models on schematic design from scratch (best model 8.15% pass on HWE-Bench), while practitioners report that an agent driving code-based EDA or headless KiCad in a loop against DRC can produce a simple board that fabs — with recurring errors in part choice, footprints and pin mapping. The reliable pattern is "agent writes, deterministic checks and datasheet-grounded review catch", not "agent designs unattended"; almost all positive numbers come from vendors.

### Cited Findings

Benchmarks and published evaluations (independent or academic)
- HWE-Bench (arXiv, March 2026): 300 real board-level design tasks in 8 domains; the model generates a schematic from requirements plus datasheets; outputs checked by electrical rule checks and LTspice transient simulation; models tested include Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok-4, Qwen3-Max, DeepSeek-v3; the best model reached an overall pass rate of 8.15%, attributed by the authors to missing physical intuition — [arXiv 2603.18102](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18102) (paper-ID-to-title mapping inferred from dates in the extract; not opened)
- PCBSchemaGen (arXiv, February 2026): 23 circuits across digital, analog and power; candidates are SKiDL Python programs scored by a deterministic structural verifier using real-IC pin- and topology-level constraints — [arXiv 2602.00510](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.00510); [Hugging Face dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Hzou9/PCBSchemaGen-Benchmarks)
- OmniSch (arXiv, spring 2026): 1,854 real schematics; tests multimodal models on converting schematic images to netlist graphs, single-shot and tool-augmented — [arXiv 2604.00270](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00270) (mapping inferred)
- PCBInnoBench (NeurIPS 2026): 372 expert-designed KiCad design-and-upgrade tasks grounded in real PCB projects, evaluating agents — [NeurIPS 2026 poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/139034) (scores not visible in extract)
- PCB-Bench (ICLR 2026): placement and routing benchmark; GPT-4o, Claude Opus 4.1 and DeepSeek-V3 formed the top tier on text tasks (i.e. it predates the newest models) — [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10009621)
- Further 2026 benchmark/agent papers exist that I saw by title only: PCBWorld ("engine-grounded PCB design automation") — [arXiv 2607.05915](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915); PCBRouteAgent ("How close are LLM agents to becoming PCB routing engineers?", November 2026) — [arXiv 2611.08175](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08175); "Agentic Electronic Design Automation: A Handoff Perspective" — [arXiv 2606.19795](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19795); an RF workflow paper where a Claude agent drives CST, ADS and KiCad by script through many simulate-and-modify iterations — [arXiv 2608.31006](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.31006)
- EEBench, an electrical-engineering agent benchmark built by the team behind atopile, scored OpenAI's "GPT-6 Astra" at 69.3% (±10%); the model is claimed to produce fully routed KiCad boards from schematics. The benchmark author sells a tool aimed at AI agents (conflict of interest), and the figure reached me via a Quilter blog post — [Quilter blog](https://www.quilter.ai/blog/llm-pcb-layout-gpt-6-astra) (vendor source; not independently confirmed)
- An LLM-agent design-review pipeline (parallel datasheet retrieval, per-group review agents, merged findings) is described academically as "the last check before fabrication" — [arXiv 2603.15672](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15672)

- Flux: positioned as cloud-collaborative, copilot-style AI-assisted design; Quilter's own comparison frames copilots as tools that "suggest placement, generate partial routes" (self-serving framing). No independent test of Flux Copilot was found — [Quilter comparison guide](https://www.quilter.ai/blog/the-2026-guide-to-autonomous-pcb-design-quilter-vs-deeppcb-vs-flux-ai)
- Diode Computers (YC S24, New York): "circuit boards as a service" — AI-driven design with modular schematics, autorouting and human review; raised an $11.4M Series A led by Andreessen Horowitz in July 2025; credits atopile and tscircuit as inspiration — [YC launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/Lee-diode-computers-inc-circuit-boards-as-a-service); [Techleap feed](https://finder.techleap.nl/news/feed/diode-computers-raises-11-4m-for-ai-pcb-design); [Dealroom](https://app.dealroom.co/companies/diode_computers/team)

- Cadence Allegro X AI: placement suggestions from netlist, thermal and signal-integrity constraints; 2026 updates reported as ML via-placement and AI-recommended decoupling. Siemens launched "Fuse", an agent coordinating its EDA suite. Altium: natural-language rule entry and an ML router are mentioned. The same sources say fully autonomous schematic-to-manufacturing design "is likely years away for complex boards" — [AtlasPCB agents post](https://atlaspcb.com/blog/ai-powered-eda-pcb-design-autonomous-agents-2026); [dev.to repost](https://dev.to/abc_8b09c7009ee0029b85665/quilter-and-siemens-push-ai-driven-pcb-design-autonomous-layout-moves-from-demo-to-production-2j9e) (secondary; feature lists unverified)
- Circuit Mind (London): now described as AI agents that check schematics against datasheets and engineering rules, with a separate QA pass on findings; a funding database lists a $25M round in October 2026 — [aifunding.me](https://aifunding.me/companies/circuit-mind) (aggregator; unverified)

Reported failure types (practitioner evidence)
- Claude driving KiCad through the community KiCAD-MCP-Server built a simple schematic, placed, routed and ran checks, but "sometimes selected the wrong footprint"; the integration is community tooling, not an official KiCad or Anthropic feature — [itechguides relay of a MakeUseOf account](https://www.itechguides.com/claude-and-kicad-what-ai-can-do-in-pcb-design/)
- A filed issue reports Claude Code repeatedly stating pins 2–4 of a MAX31856 backwards while validating a KiCad schematic; caught only when a screenshot made it obvious — [claudeissues.com #63365](https://claudeissues.com/issue/63365-bug-validating-kicad-schematic-for-exhaust-gas-sensor-using-max31856-claude-code)
- Hacker News thread on AI-assisted design for KiCad 10: one user looped a headless KiCad agent until DRC passed and the board "worked the first go at a fab house", yet the agent "still made significant mistakes about the part" and, with unusual parts, errors in package selection and footprints; another commenter concluded "no autoroute nor AI is yet even close" for doing PCB design well — [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50085553)
- A practitioner view relayed in search extracts: LLMs are "moderately good at PCB design but still very bad with high frequency signals" and "not great" on HDI and BGAs; one 2026 comparison reportedly found 168 design-rule violations in an AI layout versus zero for a professional — [StationX article](https://app.stationx.net/articles/ai-pcb-design) (exact origin of both statements not confirmed; treat as anecdote)
- A footprint defines only pad geometry, not pin assignment — a classic source of swapped transistor pins; standing advice is to check Gerbers "with the part datasheet in hand" — [Osmocom list](https://lists.osmocom.org/pipermail/simtrace/2011-June/000086.html); [KiCad forum](https://forum.kicad.info/t/error-in-the-symbol-pins-for-the-pn2222a-component/15283/9)

Automated checks and AI review tools (mostly vendor self-description)
- AllSpice "DRCY": review agent comparing schematic nets with datasheet pin definitions and block diagrams; AllSpice's own example is JTAG TDI/TDO on PB29/PB30 wired to the wrong nets; engineers keep the final call — [AllSpice post](https://www.allspice.io/post/using-ai-in-schematic-design-reviews-what-we-learned-and-what-surprised-us)
- Traceformer (review inside KiCad and Altium, automatic datasheet retrieval, per-review cost preview) — [feedbagel](https://feedbagel.com/post/traceformer-ai-powered-schematic-review-tool-for-kicad-and-altium-designer); galvano.ai (node-by-node datasheet-aware review; cannot review layouts yet) — [KiCad forum](https://forum.kicad.info/t/an-automatic-schematic-reviewer/64016); BoardMint (15 domain checkers with datasheet citations, beta) — [HN showcase](https://hnshowcase.com/post/47277903/boardmint-a-pcb-review-tool-that-avoids-ai-hallucinations); Norespin (founded 2026, flags issues with datasheet evidence before ordering) — [startuphub.ai](https://www.startuphub.ai/startups/norespin.md); Tomachie (AI PCB analysis with test-point insertion, April 2026) — [iConnect007](https://iconnect007.com/article/149788/tomachie-launches-aipowered-pcb-analysis-with-smart-test-point-insertion/149785/ein)
- Partner Electronics (UK) sells an AI-supported schematic review service that is checked by engineers — a service-shaped precedent — [Advanced Engineering UK](https://www.advancedengineeringuk.com/news/new-ai-supported-schematic-review-service-from-partner-electronics-72037/)
- A Claude-focused review guide recommends having the model extract pin numbers, nets and footprint assignments so a human verifies them, rather than trusting its verdict — [guide](https://claude-skills-guide-3lc.pages.dev/claude-code-pcb-layout-review-kicad-2026/)

### Inferences
- The reader's belief ("frontier models are much better at circuit design") is supported directionally by practitioner anecdotes and vendor benchmarks, but the only independent pass-rate I found (8.15%, models of early 2026) says unattended schematic generation is unreliable. A plan that needs the model to be right without checks is not supported by evidence.
- The reader's actual edge (agent harness engineering) maps well onto what works: code-based EDA (atopile, tscircuit, SKiDL, KiCad scripting) plus loops against ERC/DRC, SPICE, structural verifiers and a second datasheet-grounded review agent. That is a harness problem more than an electronics problem.
- Error classes that deterministic checks do not catch — wrong part for the job, wrong footprint/pin mapping, power integrity, EMC, high-speed and RF layout — are exactly where a programmer without electronics experience cannot act as the last reviewer. Variant choice should therefore favour low-speed, low-voltage, digital/sensor boards built from reference designs and pre-certified modules.
- Roughly eight AI schematic-review products appeared within about a year. Building "another review tool" is crowded; using them as a cheap second opinion is sensible.

### Gaps
- No independent head-to-head test of Flux, Quilter, JITX, Celus, CircuitMind or Diode on the same board was found. Nothing at all surfaced on JITX or Zuken AI features.
- No benchmark covering frontier-model firmware generation for microcontrollers or datasheet-reading accuracy was found in these searches.
- Scores for PCBInnoBench, PCBWorld and PCBRouteAgent were not visible; these are the most recent independent measurements and should be read directly.
- The "GPT-6 Astra / EEBench 69.3%" claim was seen only through a vendor blog extract; I could not confirm the model, the benchmark design or the number.
- Fab-side checks (JLCPCB/PCBWay DFM review, Aisler's automated checks, Eurocircuits' PCB Visualizer) were not confirmed by search. Unconfirmed prior knowledge: all major prototype fabs run free DFM/DFA checks on upload that catch manufacturability problems but not functional errors.

## 2. Prototype loop cost and speed in Europe (fabs, VAT/customs, number of spins)

### Takeaway
The loop is fast (assembled boards in days, not weeks) and cheap enough that iteration, not capital, is the constraint; since 1 July 2026 every low-value parcel from China also carries a flat €3 duty per customs line, which is immaterial for prototypes. Plan for two to three board spins per product — the commonly quoted averages are vendor-sourced.

### Cited Findings
- EU customs reform: from 1 July 2026 the duty exemption for goods under €150 is gone; postal and IOSS shipments with intrinsic value ≤ €150 pay a temporary fixed duty of €3 per customs line item, in force until 1 July 2028. VAT on imports has applied since 2021 and is unchanged — [JLCPCB notice](https://jlcpcb.com/news/eu-customs-reform-2026); [NextPCB notice](https://www.nextpcb.com/news/eu-customs-reform-2026)
- JLCPCB states that under the current IOSS framework it can collect and remit import VAT but cannot manage the temporary customs duty — [JLCPCB notice](https://jlcpcb.com/news/eu-customs-reform-2026)
- One non-PCB seller reported in September 2026 that many IOSS parcels were rejected by EU customs and recipients were asked to pay VAT and duty directly (anecdote) — [Heckin' Unicorn notice](https://heckinunicorn.com/pages/eu-taxes-duty-fees-update-sep-2026)
- JLCPCB publishes PCB assembly completion in 2–3 days (before shipping) — [JLCPCB blog](https://jlcpcb.com/blog/low-cost-and-rapid-prototype-pcb-assembly-service); a Hackaday reader noted JLCPCB's "Europackage" shipping includes pre-paid import clearance (2023) — [Hackaday comparison, Oct 2023](https://hackaday.com/2023/10/13/an-in-depth-comparison-of-hobby-pcb-manufacturers)
- Eurocircuits (EU manufacture): standard 3 working days for bare boards, 6 working days assembled; a "PCB Prototypes 3+3" service makes boards in 3 days and assembles in 3 — [Eurocircuits proto page](https://www.eurocircuits.com/pcb-assembly-proto/); [Eurocircuits 3+3](https://eurocircuits.com/?p=8922)
- Relative prices (forum anecdotes, not price lists): Eurocircuits "two to four times the rates of Aisler" — [EEVblog thread](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/manufacture/single-run-prototype-pcb-manufacture/); Aisler "3–4x JLC" and arriving around the same time — [PedalPCB forum](https://forum.pedalpcb.com/goto/post?id=224934)
- Czech contract assembler SAFIRAL advertises express assembly from two working days, prototypes in about ten working days, three or more weeks for larger orders — [dps-az.cz](https://dps-az.cz/vyroba/id:29197/expresni-vyroba-elektroniky)
- Spins: a trade article states boards undergo on average 2.9 respins, each costing $28,482 and 16 days (underlying study not named; figures are for corporate projects) — [Dataweek](https://dataweek.co.za/23008r); EMA puts a respin of a moderately complex high-speed board at $10,000–$50,000 — [EMA blog](https://www.ema-eda.com/ema-resources/blog/cost-of-pcb-design-spins-emd/); a 2025 PCD&F/Circuits Assembly survey found nearly 60% of designers produce one to five respins a year — [Cadence resources](https://resources.pcb.cadence.com/blog/why-pcb-data-management-is-an-engineering-problem) (source page for the survey figure inferred); Siemens claims DFM tools cut respins by 57% on average (vendor claim) — [Siemens fact sheet](https://resources.sw.siemens.com/da-DK/fact-sheet-xpedition-design-and-manufacturing/)

### Inferences
- For a simple board the solo-founder respin cost is a few hundred euros and one to two weeks, two orders of magnitude below the corporate figures quoted above (which include engineering time and schedule loss). The real cost of a wrong board is calendar time and attention.
- With assembled prototypes arriving in roughly one to two weeks door to door, three spins fit into six to eight weeks — compatible with the reader's "test with real customers within weeks" requirement for simple devices.
- An EU fab (Aisler, Eurocircuits, a Czech assembler) removes customs friction and gives an "assembled in EU" story at two to ten times the unit price; sensible for small production batches where customers are B2B, less so for first spins.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm a 2026 price for a small assembled batch delivered to Czechia from any fab. Unconfirmed prior knowledge: five to ten assembled 2- or 4-layer boards with common parts from JLCPCB typically land at roughly €60–200 including shipping and VAT; Aisler and Eurocircuits are several times that. Verify with each vendor's calculator.
- Whether JLCPCB/PCBWay DDP shipping lines now prepay the €3 duty, or the carrier bills the recipient with a handling fee, was not settled by the sources.
- PCBWay and Elecrow lead times and prices were not retrieved.
- No independent statistic on first-pass success for small hobby or indie boards was found; the "70% fail first run" figure seen in one blog is unsourced — [Gighz blog](https://gighz.net/?p=19851).

## 3. EU compliance for a small electronics product

### Takeaway
One person can legally place a low-voltage electronic product on the EU market by self-declaration (CE) for EMC, RoHS and — with harmonised standards fully applied — radio and radio-cybersecurity, but self-declaration means owning the technical file and the liability, not skipping the testing. The only explicit carve-out is narrow (custom-built evaluation kits for professionals used solely in R&D facilities), and the recurring cost that scales with ambition is per-country producer registration (WEEE, packaging, batteries), not the lab.

### Cited Findings

Exemptions for kits and development boards
- The EMC Directive (2014/30/EU, Article 2(2)(e) as cited by a forum poster) excludes "custom built evaluation kits destined for professionals to be used solely at research and development facilities for such purposes" — [Conformance](https://www.conformance.co.uk/ce-legislation-information/electromagnetic-compatibility-emc-directive); [Olimex forum](https://www.olimex.com/forum/index.php?msg=25108)
- Large vendors lean on this: Digilent states its development and evaluation kits "do not fall within the scope of the EMC Directive" because they are not finished goods and are for internal engineering evaluation in R&D laboratory environments; Infineon kit guides say boards "may therefore not comply with CE or similar standards" — [Digilent EMC disclaimer](https://assets.testequity.com/te1/Documents/pdf/digilent/Digilent_EMC_DISCLAIMER_DIGILENT_DEVELOPMENT_AND_EVALUATION_KITS_0526.pdf); [Infineon kit guide](https://mouser.com/datasheet/2/196/Infineon_KIT_XMC7200_DC_V1_motor_drive_card_guide_-3518038.pdf)
- A forum rule of thumb (opinion, not law): if meaningful EMC tests can be performed on a product, it needs to comply and be CE marked unless a specific exclusion applies — [electrondepot thread](https://www.electrondepot.com/electrodesign/ce-testing-893040-1.htm)
- Raspberry Pi published its own account of compliance testing for a bare board product (content not read; listed as a reference for a board-level product that chose full compliance) — [Raspberry Pi news](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/compliance-testing/)

Radio and radio cybersecurity
- For the RED cybersecurity requirements (Articles 3.3(d)(e)(f), EN 18031 series): self-assessment is possible only if the manufacturer fully applies EN 18031 and none of the Commission's restrictions applies; otherwise a notified body is required; Nemko says a notified body is "for all practical purposes required" for EN 18031-3 — [Applus FAQ](https://www.appluslaboratories.com/global/en/red-cybersecurity-faqs); [Nemko](https://www.nemko.com/hubfs/Cybersecurity.pdf)
- A pre-certified, secure radio module supports compliance but "does not replace the need for end-product certification" — [Espressif EN 18031 guide](https://developer.espressif.com/blog/2025/04/esp32-red-da-en18031-compliance-guide/)
- One case study: an expedited notified-body assessment after a failed launch cost about three times a pre-launch gap analysis (no absolute figures) — [TecEx case study](https://tecexglobalcompliance-site.pages.dev/blog/electronics-red-cybersecurity-case-study/)
- The RED cybersecurity delegated rules are due to be repealed on 11 December 2027, when the Cyber Resilience Act takes over — [Qualysec](https://qualysec.com/red-cybersecurity-requirements/)

Cyber Resilience Act
- CRA reporting obligations (Article 14) apply from 11 September 2026: actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents must be reported to ENISA and the national CSIRT on a 24-hour / 72-hour / 14-day cascade via the Single Reporting Platform; this covers products already on the market. The rest of the CRA applies from 11 December 2027 — [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/its-live-the-cyber-resilience-act-reporting-is-mandatory-as-of-today-11-september-2026); [McCann FitzGerald](https://www.mccannfitzgerald.com/knowledge/data-privacy-and-cyber-risk/cyber-resilience-act-reporting-obligations-apply-from-11-september-2026)
- Breach of reporting obligations can attract fines up to €15 million or 2.5% of worldwide turnover; the Commission published application guidance on 27 July 2026 — [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/07/eu-cyber-resilience-act-24hour-reporting-duties-start-september-11-2026); [Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/alerts-lifesciences-technology-preparing-for-eu-cyber-resilience-act)

Liability
- The new Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 must be transposed by 9 December 2026 and applies to products placed on the market after that date; it extends strict liability to software, updates and AI systems, adds disclosure obligations and rebuttable presumptions that ease the claimant's proof, and extends liability to further economic operators such as authorised representatives — [Buse](https://buse.de/en/blog-en/commercial-en/what-will-change-with-the-new-eu-product-liability-directive-eu-2024-2853/); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/ita/legal-updates/eu-directive-2024-2853-the-new-product-liability-regime-for-defective-products); [Clayton Utz, Sept 2026](https://www.claytonutz.com/insights/2026/september/time-to-prepare-europe-s-product-liability-changes-and-what-they-mean-for-australian-manufacturers)

Test costs
- Germany, indicative 2026 prices from a marketing blog about EMC lab websites (not a lab price list): pre-compliance exploratory test about €850–1,200 for half a day excl. VAT; single standards from about €700 (ESD, EN 61000-4-2) to about €4,000 for EN 55032 emissions over one to two days; anechoic chamber billed around €2,200 per day — [Kolonell blog](https://kolonell.com/en/blog/emc-testing-laboratory-website-cost-berlin-2026)
- General (US-oriented) figures: off-site EMC lab time $2,000–2,500 per day in one source and "up to $10,000 per day" per Keysight; Tektronix estimates $5,000–10,000 for FCC plus CE certification of an unintentional radiator, excluding engineering time — [Signal Integrity Journal](https://signalintegrityjournal.com/articles/1633-addressing-emc-challenges-with-in-house-emc-pre-compliance-testing); [Keysight](https://www.keysight.com/used/ad/en/knowledge/guides/emc-testing); [Tektronix](https://www.tek.com.cn/blog/financial-case-emi-emc-pre-compliance-test-solution)
- Würth Elektronik and Keysight (Böblingen, DAkkS-accredited) offer pay-per-use pre-compliance lab access; no rates shown — [Würth EMC lab brochure](https://www.we-online.com/files/pdf1/we-eisos_broschuere_emv-labor_210x297_en_2024-12_screen.pdf); [Keysight flyer](https://www.keysight.com/au/en/assets/7018-06450/flyers/5992-3572.pdf)
- A Czech contract assembler (ATM Elektro) also lists EMC testing among its services — [ATM Elektro](https://www.atmelektro.cz/)

WEEE, packaging, selling into several countries
- There is no single EU-wide WEEE registration; registration is per member state, and a seller without an establishment in the country must appoint an authorised representative there — [Compliance Gate](https://compliancegate.com/electronic-waste-management-european-union)
- Germany: Stiftung EAR base fee from €12.40, determination fee €70–2,036 depending on case, plus an insolvency-proof guarantee for B2C devices (figures relayed by service vendors, not seen on the EAR fee schedule) — [ProfitPath](https://profitpath.com/en/tools/weee-registration); [EPR vendor page](https://epr-website.pages.dev/weee-registration-germany)
- Service pricing (sales pages): one provider charges £995 per country for registration, authorised representative, scheme contract and certificate; another £2,985 onboarding plus £585 per month for three EU countries — [EPR vendor pricing](https://epr-website.pages.dev/pricing); authorised-representative services also from [ecosistant](https://www.ecosistant.eu/en/services/weee-authorized-representative)
- Amazon requires a WEEE registration number for in-scope products on its .de, .fr, .es and .it marketplaces — [EPR vendor page](https://epr-website.pages.dev/data-centre/amazon-epr-compliance/amazon-pay-on-behalf-vs-weee-registration); German packaging (LUCID registration plus dual-system contract) is a separate obligation — [eBay seller help](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/extended-producer-responsibility-business-sellers/german-packaging-act-business-sellers?id=5336)

### Inferences
- The legally cleanest way to sell within weeks is B2B, wired (no radio), low-voltage, externally powered through a bought-in CE-marked supply, and marketed as an evaluation or development tool for professionals. This minimises (does not eliminate) EMC/LVD exposure, keeps the product outside the consumer-facing regimes, and avoids RED and battery rules entirely. The exclusion wording ("custom built", "solely at R&D facilities") does not obviously cover a catalogue product sold to hobbyists, so relying on it for a web shop open to consumers is a legal risk the seller carries.
- Adding a radio moves the product into RED including cybersecurity; using a pre-certified module reduces radio testing but, per Espressif, the end product still needs its own assessment. A first product should not have a radio unless the niche demands it.
- A realistic compliance budget for a first simple wired product, on the German indicative rates above, is one pre-compliance half-day (about €1,000) plus, if selling at volume, a formal EMC emissions/immunity run in the low thousands of euros. That is small relative to the robot-cell route the reader rejected, and no outside licensed partner is structurally required.
- Per-country WEEE/packaging registration is the main reason small EU hardware sellers either ship from one country to businesses only, restrict the countries they ship to, or sell through a distributor/marketplace that acts as producer. At roughly £1,000 per country via a service provider, serving ten countries B2C costs more than the lab.
- Variant (b) and (c) shift most of this to the client: a design-service provider or fixture builder supplying a one-off to a manufacturer is not placing a series product on the consumer market. Liability then runs through contract (specification, acceptance test, limitation of liability) rather than product law — though custom test equipment handed to a factory is still equipment put into service, and mains-powered or pneumatic fixtures raise LVD/machinery questions that I did not research.
- CRA is now a live obligation for any product with digital elements that a solo maker has already shipped (reporting since 11 September 2026), and from December 2027 it adds security-by-design, vulnerability handling and a support-period commitment — a recurring burden for a one-person firm with connected products.

### Gaps
- Not confirmed by search (unconfirmed prior knowledge, verify before relying): the CRA exempts micro and small enterprises from fines for missing the 24-hour early-warning deadline and allows them a simplified technical-documentation format, but otherwise has no small-firm exemption; products "made available" before 11 December 2027 are outside the main obligations unless substantially modified.
- General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988: nothing retrieved. Unconfirmed prior knowledge: applies since 13 December 2024 to consumer products, requires an EU-established responsible economic operator, an internal risk analysis and technical documentation, and adds online-marketplace duties; products intended only for professionals are outside it unless consumers can foreseeably use them.
- Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542: nothing retrieved. Unconfirmed prior knowledge: per-country battery producer registration, labelling duties, and user-removable portable batteries from February 2027 — strong reasons to avoid a built-in battery in a first product.
- RoHS and REACH: nothing retrieved; unconfirmed prior knowledge is that RoHS is self-declared on the basis of supplier declarations (JLCPCB/LCSC parts carry RoHS statements) and REACH requires SVHC communication above 0.1%.
- Low Voltage Directive scope (50–1000 V AC / 75–1500 V DC) and the consequence that sub-50 V devices fall outside it (but under GPSR if consumer-facing) was not confirmed by search.
- Czech specifics not found: EMC lab day rates in Czechia; the fee and procedure for registering as a producer of electrical equipment with the Czech Ministry of Environment and joining a collective scheme (ASEKOL, ELEKTROWIN, REMA, RETELA); Czech packaging (EKO-KOM) thresholds; cost of product-liability insurance for a Czech sole trader or s.r.o.
- Current harmonisation status of EN 18031 was inconsistent across sources (one module vendor's FAQ still says a notified body is required because the standards are not harmonised; it looks outdated) — [Ezurio FAQ](https://ezurio.com/support/faqs/will-a-notified-body-be-required-to-review-conformity-to-red-cyber). Check the Official Journal.

## 4. Demand tests and channels; examples of solo hardware businesses (variant a)

### Takeaway
Channels for a quick demand test exist and are cheap to enter (Crowd Supply, Tindie, own shop), but the one well-documented indie case shows the economics: TinyPilot reached about $1M in sales within three years yet made almost no profit until its third full year, and needed a small team by then. Niche hardware is a workable small business, rarely a high-margin solo one.

### Cited Findings
- Crowd Supply (owned by Mouser Electronics) states that over 90% of projects launched on it reach their funding goal; its campaign fee is typically 12% of campaign sales, no equity — [Crowd Supply apply page](https://crowdsupply.com/apply); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowd_Supply). These are self-reported, and the platform curates which projects launch.
- Older Crowd Supply data: 50% success (2013, company blog); 57.8% of projects met goals (2015) against about 30% for comparable Kickstarter categories at the time; average campaign raised $52,200 (mid-2010s) — [Crowd Supply blog 2013](https://blog.crowdsupply.com/2013/09/19/the-first-six-months/); [The Next Web 2015](https://thenextweb.com/news/crowd-supply-is-succeeding-where-kickstarter-and-indiegogo-are-failing-miserably); [Oregon Business](https://oregonbusiness.com/12169-crowd-supply-raises-money-for-itself/)
- A behind-the-scenes account of a successful hardware crowdfunding campaign exists from the Wokwi founder (content not read) — [Wokwi blog](https://blog.wokwi.com/hardware-crowdfunding-behind-the-scenes/)
- Tindie: a 2025 blog reports more than 468,000 orders shipped and over 19,500 products listed (small unverified blog); fees reported as 5% commission plus 2–3% payment processing (secondary, possibly dated) — [flyemu](https://flyemu.com/what-is-tindie-the-marketplace-for-indie-hardware-makers/); [Technowize](https://www.technowize.com/tindie-the-largest-collection-of-open-source-hardware-on-the-planet/)
- A Raspberry Pi magazine author describes a Tindie shop that "slowly built up to become a small part of my regular income" — [Raspberry Pi magazine](https://magazine.raspberrypi.com/articles/do-the-hustle)
- TinyPilot (KVM-over-IP device built on Raspberry Pi, started by one software developer, Michael Lynch; covered as a solo-developer product in 2021 — [Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2021/11/02/hardware-project-becomes-successful-product-for-solo-developer)):
  - mid-2021: $30k monthly revenue, "barely covers costs"; a 5% margin called unhealthy against the 50–60% he understood physical products usually earn — [retrospective, June 2021](https://mtlynch.io/retrospectives/2021/06)
  - April 2022: $58k per month in revenue yet losing money; materials about 30% of revenue; hardware redesign costs — [retrospective, May 2022](https://mtlynch.io/retrospectives/2022/05/)
  - 2022: $807,458 sales, $10,447 net profit ("basically breaking even for two years"); 2023: about $997k sales and $236k profit, credited to a metal enclosure that supported higher prices — [year-6 retrospective](https://mtlynch.io/solo-developer-year-6)
  - sold in 2024 for $600K; by then he worked with a team including three other developers — [podcast summary](https://metacast.app/podcast/software-misadventures/2CNdI2Zt/growing-and-selling-an-indie-business-michael-lynch-tinypilot/OfyqGNb6)
- An academic study of open-source hardware entrepreneurs' motivations exists (useful for the "why people do this despite thin margins" question; content not read) — [Design Science journal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/design-science/article/understanding-the-motivations-for-opensource-hardware-entrepreneurship/34B8D98DA878CAF5160892DC7258CA41)

### Inferences
- First proof for variant (a) is reachable in weeks and for low capital: one or two prototype spins (hundreds of euros), a landing page or Crowd Supply pre-launch page, and a pre-order test. Crowd Supply's curation, Mouser fulfilment and 12% fee make it the natural test for an engineer-facing niche tool; Tindie suits tiny volumes.
- TinyPilot shows the pattern to expect: demand appears early; profit appears late; margin came from product decisions (enclosure, price), not from the electronics; and a product that works needs staff for fulfilment and support. It was also built on an existing computer module, not a from-scratch board — the lowest-risk design style.
- AI-assisted design lowers the cost of creating a board; it does not change margins, support, inventory or compliance. Since anyone can now generate a simple board, the durable advantage in variant (a) is niche knowledge, firmware/software quality and documentation, brand trust among a specific buyer group, and an installed base — which is where a strong software engineer has an edge.
- Likely good niches for this founder are those where the product is mostly software wrapped in a simple board: protocol adapters and bus tools, data loggers with good host software, lab automation interfaces, test and debug tools for firmware teams.

### Gaps
- No current Kickstarter figures were retrieved. Unconfirmed prior knowledge: Kickstarter's own statistics page has long shown technology as one of its lowest-success categories, at roughly one in five projects funded.
- No reliable income distribution for Tindie, Lectronz or Elecrow sellers, and no data on Amazon for niche boards, was found. Aggregator revenue estimates for Tindie and Crowd Supply themselves ([Owler](https://www.owler.com/company/tindie)) are not trustworthy.
- Only one solo-origin hardware business with published financials was confirmed. Other indie sellers that would be worth checking by name (not searched): Great Scott Gadgets, Unexpected Maker, ANAVI, Olimex (Bulgaria), 1BitSquared, Excamera Labs, Apollo Automation, SMLIGHT.
- No Czech or Central European indie-hardware revenue example was found.
- Typical unit margins after fulfilment, returns and payment fees for sub-€100 boards were not found beyond the TinyPilot data.

## 5. Custom electronics design as a fast-turnaround service (variant b)

### Takeaway
There is an established, price-transparent market: German freelance hardware/PCB designers bill roughly €80–110 per hour, global marketplaces sell simple layouts for a few hundred dollars, and design-house projects run into five figures over several weeks. A venture-funded AI-first competitor (Diode Computers) already sells "circuit boards as a service" with human review, so speed alone is not a moat, and the newcomer's lack of electronics judgement is the main obstacle to being trusted with a client's board.

### Cited Findings
- Germany: freelancermap shows an average of €82/hour across Altium Designer freelancer profiles; €90/hour average in one regional sample of embedded/electronics profiles; a hardware project near Mannheim listed €110/hour (to mid-2025). For comparison, software developers on the same platform have a median of €90/hour (September 2026), half of profiles between €80 and €100 — [freelancermap Altium](https://www.freelancermap.de/freelancer/altium-designer); [freelancermap Lahr](https://www.freelancermap.de/freelancer/lahr); [freelance.de project](https://freelance.de/Projekte/Projekt-1157631-Hardware-Developer-Advanced-Semiconductor-Embedded-Systems-Project); [freelancermap rate article](https://www.freelancermap.de/blog/?p=72121)
- Marketplaces: Fiverr's guide lists PCB work at about $186 typical fixed price and $45–80/hour, delivery 5–21 days — [Fiverr guide](https://fiverr.com/resources/guides/costs/electronics-design-engineer); a CadCrowd freelancer offers layout tiers at $120 (1–2 layers, up to 40 components), $300 and $700 — [CadCrowd listing](https://www.cadcrowd.com/service/1371-i-will-design-professional-pcb-layouts-schematics)
- A CadCrowd cost example for a full project: about 252 hours and $25,200 at $100/hour (schematic 40 h, library 20 h, layout 60 h, routing 120 h, DFM 12 h) — [CadCrowd blog](https://www.cadcrowd.com/blog/?p=40265)
- A vendor guide prices an "intermediate" 4–6 layer board such as a simple microcontroller board at $1,000–3,000 for layout — [AnyPCBA guide](https://anypcba.com/decoding-pcb-layout-services-cost-a-comprehensive-guide-for-2024/)
- Beta Layout (Germany) quotes layout services in 22 or 28 working days — [Beta Layout](https://nl.beta-layout.com/pcb/layout/specificaties/)
- AI-first competitor: Diode Computers sells boards as a service (AI design, autorouting, human review), with $11.4M Series A funding in 2025 — [YC launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/Lee-diode-computers-inc-circuit-boards-as-a-service); [Techleap feed](https://finder.techleap.nl/news/feed/diode-computers-raises-11-4m-for-ai-pcb-design)
- AI-assisted service precedent in Europe: Partner Electronics (UK) offers AI-supported schematic review checked by engineers — [Advanced Engineering UK](https://www.advancedengineeringuk.com/news/new-ai-supported-schematic-review-service-from-partner-electronics-72037/)
- Czech contract manufacturers bundle development with production (e.g. ADDAT offers "complete EMS services" from prototype to series; AŽD offers assembly services) — these are both potential competitors and potential referral channels — [ADDAT](https://www.addat.cz/zakazkova-vyroba-elektroniky); [AŽD](https://www.azd.cz/cs/sluzby/osazovani-dps-a-montaz-elektronickych-sestav)
- A practitioner discussion on how to price design projects exists in the Fedevel forum (Central European PCB-design training community; content not read) — [Fedevel forum](https://courses.fedevel.com/forum/course-questions/2472-how-to-price-projects)

### Inferences
- Time to first paid job can be short (a fixed-price small board for a local firm or via a marketplace), and capital need is near zero. But the first paying client is buying responsibility for a working board. With little hardware experience, an AI-first solo shop either limits itself to low-risk classes (breakout and adapter boards, microcontroller plus sensors, redesigns for component obsolescence, test jigs) or pays an experienced reviewer per project — which reintroduces the outside dependency the reader wants to avoid, though as a paid review of a few hours, not a partnership.
- The price ceiling is set by €80–110/hour German freelancers and far cheaper global marketplaces. An AI-first shop wins on calendar time and fixed price, not on hourly rate: for example a fixed €2–5k for schematic, layout, firmware skeleton and five assembled prototypes in two weeks compares well with the 22–28 working days quoted for layout alone. (Illustrative positioning, not a sourced price.)
- Liability sits in the contract, and compliance of the end product stays with the client — the lightest regulatory position of the three variants.
- Durable advantage is weak unless the work compounds: a library of verified, reusable design blocks (in code-based EDA), a private regression/review harness, and repeat clients. Otherwise it is hourly work in a market where AI is lowering the price of exactly this labour, and where clients' own engineers get the same tools.
- This variant is also the fastest way to gain the hardware competence needed for (a) and (c) while being paid.

### Gaps
- Czech rates were not found (no figures for Czech freelance electronics designers or design houses). Unconfirmed expectation: materially below German rates.
- No data on where small design shops get clients (referrals from EMS firms and fabs, marketplaces, LinkedIn) beyond the general observation above.
- No evidence on whether clients accept AI-designed boards, or on how AI-first shops price (fixed versus hourly) — Diode's prices were not visible.
- No evidence was found for other AI-first design shops in Europe; absence in these searches is not proof of absence.
- Professional-indemnity insurance cost and typical liability caps for design contracts were not researched.

## 6. Test fixtures and end-of-line testers (variant c)

### Takeaway
Fixtures are a real, recurring, per-product purchase (roughly $2k–20k each, 2–6 weeks lead time, required for every new board that goes into volume) and the work is mostly software plus simple mechanics — a good skills match — but I found no evidence on whether small Central European EMS firms outsource test development, and the Czech search surfaced university theses building such testers for companies, a sign that in-house or student labour is a common substitute.

### Cited Findings
- Fixture prices (vendor and assembler blogs, not neutral data): tooling $2,000–$20,000+, higher for clamshell fixtures and high node counts; an example of a $15,000 bed-of-nails clamshell; "several thousand US dollars per product"; older IPC material mentions upwards of $60,000 for a large board on a traditional ICT system — [AllPCB](https://www.allpcb.com/allelectrohub/flying-probe-vs-fixture-testing-cost); [Bester PCBA](https://www.besterpcba.com/low-volume-hardware-flexible-testing); [PCBCart](https://www.pcbcart.com/article/content/ict-vs-flying-probe-testing-guide.html); [IPC paper](https://www.ipc.org/system/files/technical_resource/E7%26S13_02.pdf) (which blog gave which figure is not certain from the extract)
- Lead times: 2–4 weeks to 4–6 weeks typical for build and debug; a simple modular bed-of-nails in roughly 1–3 weeks; heavier custom fixtures 6–10+ weeks — [Bester PCBA fixture service](https://www.besterpcba.com/fixture-service-midvolume-test); [AllPCB](https://www.allpcb.com/allelectrohub/cost-effective-ict-testing-strategies-for-optimizing-test-fixture-design)
- A bed-of-nails test takes under 10 seconds per board versus minutes for flying probe; break-even against flying probe is put at about 500–1,000 units by one vendor and 200–300 units by another — [JLCPCB blog](https://jlcpcb.com/blog/bed-of-nails-pcb-testing); [AllPCB](https://www.allpcb.com/allelectrohub/flying-probe-vs-fixture-testing-cost)
- Positioning used by an assembler: "bed-of-nails fixtures as a service" for mid-volume teams that cannot wait, and a warning that over-specified test coverage produces "the $40,000 paperweight" for low-volume hardware — [Bester PCBA](https://www.besterpcba.com/fixture-service-midvolume-test); [Bester PCBA](https://www.besterpcba.com/low-volume-hardware-flexible-testing)
- Fixture kits: Ingun sells modular test-fixture kits that customers customise; its manual MA series (MA 160/F, 260/F, 350/F, 360/F) targets small-volume PCB testing — [Ingun](https://ingun.com/en/News/Quality/Pruefadapter); [Dataweek](https://dataweek.co.za/16079r). Spring probes are bought separately and rated for roughly 100,000 to 1,000,000 cycles — [Promax](https://promaxpogopin.com/blog/test-probe/pogo-pin-test-fixture/)
- Open frameworks: OpenHTF is Google's open-source Python library for hardware test (phases as Python functions, "plugs" wrapping instruments, pass/fail against measurement limits; version 1.6.1 on PyPI) — [GitHub](https://github.com/google/openhtf); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/openhtf/); a commercial layer (Nominal Connect) runs OpenHTF and other Python scripts with real-time visualisation — [Nominal](https://nominalinc.substack.com/p/hardware-testing-with-openhtf-and); a separate small project, LibreHTF, exists — [GitHub](https://github.com/mcpcpc/librehtf)
- AI tooling is entering test design: Tomachie launched AI PCB analysis with automatic test-point insertion in April 2026 — [iConnect007](https://iconnect007.com/article/149788/tomachie-launches-aipowered-pcb-analysis-with-smart-test-point-insertion/149785/ein)
- European EMS market: €57 billion record in 2023, double-digit decline in 2024, slight growth expected for 2025 (in4ma, via EMSNOW headlines) — [EMSNOW](https://www.emsnow.com/category/ems/page/173/); [EMSNOW](https://www.emsnow.com/category/ems/page/104/). in4ma's half-year 2025 analysis covered 177 EMS companies (219 legal entities) with about €4.5 billion revenue in H1 2025; Eastern European results were weaker as some Western firms moved production back west — [Evertiq, Oct 2025](https://evertiq.com/news/2025-10-10-ems-industry-sees-decline-and-stagnation-across-europe). in4ma/EMSNOW also published a 2025 tour report on Central and Eastern European EMS (not read) — [EMSNOW](https://www.emsnow.com/category/ems/page/73/)
- Czech small and mid-sized contract assemblers found (own websites, a sample, not a census): SAFIRAL, ELO+ (small to medium series), MARPOS (Ostrava; assembly and device testing), ADDAT (Liberec), ELITRONIC production (three SMT lines, seven pick-and-place machines), ATM Elektro, ELSO Ostrava, AŽD — [SAFIRAL](https://www.safiral.cz/ems); [ELO+](https://www.elo.cz/osazovani-dps); [MARPOS](https://www.khkmsk.cz/clenove/marpos-s-r-o/); [ADDAT](https://www.addat.cz/zakazkova-vyroba-elektroniky); [ELITRONIC](https://www.elitronic.cz/); [ATM Elektro](https://www.atmelektro.cz/); [ELSO](http://elso-ostrava.cz/)

### Inferences
- Fit with the reader's constraints is the best of the three on paper: the customer is a business; each job is a one-off delivered against an acceptance test; the fixture is not a series product placed on the consumer market; low-voltage fixtures built on a bought-in mechanical kit need no licensed partner; and the core value is Python test software, logging, traceability and firmware flashing — the reader's home ground. A first proof (one paid fixture for a local assembler or product company) is plausible within weeks at a capital outlay of a kit, probes and instruments in the low thousands of euros.
- AI helps where it is most reliable: generating test sequences, instrument drivers and report code from a board's schematic and test specification, and generating the probe plate from the board's test-point coordinates. Mistakes are caught on the bench against known-good and known-bad boards, a far more forgiving loop than ordering a wrong PCB.
- The buyers who feel the pain most are probably not large EMS plants (they have test engineering departments and ICT machines) but small EMS firms and product companies making hundreds to a few thousand units, where a $15k fixture is unjustifiable and testing is manual. This is inference; no source confirmed it for Central Europe.
- What accumulates: a reusable test-station platform (hardware interface board, software stack, database of results), and switching costs once a client's products all run on it. A fixture-plus-software platform sold repeatedly is closer to a product than (b), and a client's production data makes the supplier sticky.
- What stops a newcomer: trust (a tester that passes bad boards or stops a line is costly), on-site debugging at the customer, mechanical precision, and the fact that each fixture is bespoke work — it scales with hours unless the platform is standardised. A weak European EMS market in 2024–2025 also means tight budgets, though it favours cheap fixtures over expensive ones.
- Hybrid path: variant (c) can begin as a by-product of (a) or (b) — every board one designs needs a test jig — and the same jig platform can then be sold to others.

### Gaps
- Not found: the number of EMS firms in Czechia or Central Europe, national market size, or a ranking of major names (in4ma and Reed data sit behind paywalls). Unconfirmed prior knowledge: large foreign-owned plants in Czechia include Foxconn (Pardubice, Kutná Hora), Jabil-type and Tier-1 automotive electronics sites, alongside a long tail of small domestic assemblers such as those listed above.
- Not found: any evidence on whether small EMS firms outsource functional test development, what they pay, or who the Czech/Slovak/Polish fixture builders are. This is the decisive unknown for variant (c) and is best resolved by ten phone calls to the assemblers listed above.
- Not found in searches: Merifix kits, 3D-printed fixture projects (e.g. OpenFixture-type designs), pytest-based production test stacks (HardPy), or hosted test-data services (TofuPilot). Unconfirmed prior knowledge: all exist; low-cost Chinese lever-type fixture kits sell for well under €200 and are widely used by indie makers.
- European (as opposed to US/Asian vendor-blog) fixture prices and lead times were not found.
- Safety/compliance obligations for custom test equipment supplied to a factory (LVD, Machinery Regulation for pneumatic presses, employer's work-equipment rules) were not researched.

## 7. Failure modes

### Takeaway
The documented ways this fails are economic and operational more than technical: thin margins after materials, redesigns and staff; compliance and per-country registration costs that arrive when sales expand; AI design errors in part choice and pin mapping that pass automated checks; and new continuing liabilities (CRA reporting, software-inclusive product liability) attached to every unit already shipped.

### Cited Findings
- Thin margins despite strong sales: TinyPilot had $58k monthly revenue and still lost money in April 2022, with materials at about 30% of revenue and hardware redesigns as a cost; two years of roughly break-even at about $800k annual sales — [retrospective, May 2022](https://mtlynch.io/retrospectives/2022/05/); [year-6 retrospective](https://mtlynch.io/solo-developer-year-6)
- A "solo" hardware product stops being solo: TinyPilot's founder worked with a team including three other developers before the sale — [podcast summary](https://metacast.app/podcast/software-misadventures/2CNdI2Zt/growing-and-selling-an-indie-business-michael-lynch-tinypilot/OfyqGNb6)
- AI design errors that pass checks: wrong footprint selection, reversed pin numbering read from a datasheet, wrong package for unusual parts, weakness on high-frequency, HDI and BGA designs — [itechguides](https://www.itechguides.com/claude-and-kicad-what-ai-can-do-in-pcb-design/); [claudeissues.com](https://claudeissues.com/issue/63365-bug-validating-kicad-schematic-for-exhaust-gas-sensor-using-max31856-claude-code); [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50085553)
- Independent benchmark reality check: best frontier model 8.15% overall pass on from-scratch board-level schematic tasks (early 2026 models) — [arXiv 2603.18102](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18102)
- Respins cost time: 16 days per respin in the commonly cited (unsourced) industry figure — [Dataweek](https://dataweek.co.za/23008r)
- Compliance cost surprise when fixing late: a notified-body route after a failed launch cost about three times a pre-launch gap analysis; four EU markets were affected by a voluntary sales suspension in that case — [TecEx case study](https://tecexglobalcompliance-site.pages.dev/blog/electronics-red-cybersecurity-case-study/)
- Multi-country selling multiplies registrations: no EU-wide WEEE registration; about £995 per country via one service provider; marketplaces require the numbers — [Compliance Gate](https://compliancegate.com/electronic-waste-management-european-union); [EPR vendor pricing](https://epr-website.pages.dev/pricing)
- Liabilities attached to shipped units: CRA reporting duties since 11 September 2026 cover products already sold, with fines up to €15M or 2.5% of turnover; the new Product Liability Directive (from December 2026) covers software and updates and eases the claimant's burden of proof — [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/its-live-the-cyber-resilience-act-reporting-is-mandatory-as-of-today-11-september-2026); [Buse](https://buse.de/en/blog-en/commercial-en/what-will-change-with-the-new-eu-product-liability-directive-eu-2024-2853/)
- Import friction: IOSS parcels rejected and recipients billed directly (one seller's report, September 2026) — [Heckin' Unicorn](https://heckinunicorn.com/pages/eu-taxes-duty-fees-update-sep-2026)
- Over-investment in test: full-coverage fixtures bought for low-volume products become "the $40,000 paperweight" — [Bester PCBA](https://www.besterpcba.com/low-volume-hardware-flexible-testing)
- Market cycle for variant (c): European EMS revenue fell by double digits in 2024 and stagnated into 2025, weakest in Eastern Europe — [Evertiq](https://evertiq.com/news/2025-10-10-ems-industry-sees-decline-and-stagnation-across-europe)

### Inferences
Assessment of the three variants against the reader's seven criteria (my synthesis of sections 1–6; not sourced statements):

| Criterion | (a) Niche product sold online | (b) Fast custom design service | (c) Test fixtures / EOL testers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Doable alone, legally | Yes by self-declaration; founder is the manufacturer and carries CE, WEEE/packaging per country, CRA, product liability. Lightest if B2B, wired, no battery, no radio | Yes; lightest regime — contract liability, client owns product compliance | Yes for low-voltage fixtures; contract liability; equipment-safety questions if mains or pneumatics |
| 2. Capital and time to first proof | Hundreds to low thousands of euros; pre-orders in 4–10 weeks; real profit can take years | Near zero capital; first paid job in weeks if a client is found | Low thousands of euros for kit and instruments; first paid fixture in weeks if a client is found |
| 3. Share of work AI can do | High for simple boards and firmware, with check loops; human must own part choice and pin mapping | Same, but errors land on a client's schedule | Highest — mostly Python and simple interface boards; bench catches errors |
| 4. Demand, prices, margins | Proven niches exist; margins thin (TinyPilot) | Proven; €80–110/h benchmark, falling with AI | Fixtures $2k–20k each; local demand unverified |
| 5. Competition / barriers | Low entry barriers; clones; many makers | Freelancers, design houses, EMS in-house design, Diode | In-house test engineers, fixture houses, students; trust barrier |
| 6. Durable advantage | Brand, software, installed base in a niche | Weak unless design-block library and repeat clients | Test platform and client lock-in |
| 7. Typical failure | Break-even treadmill, inventory, support, compliance spread | Bad board for a client; price pressure; hourly ceiling | No outsourcing demand; bespoke work that does not scale |

- All three avoid what made the robot-cell option unattractive: no licensed third party is structurally required, liability is bounded (low voltage, no machinery in the default case), and a first proof is possible in weeks.
- The shared weak point is the founder's missing electronics judgement at the exact step where AI is least reliable. The cheapest mitigation is to choose board classes where a mistake costs one respin (not a client or a field failure) and to buy a few hours of experienced design review per board until competence accumulates.
- The lowest-risk sequence consistent with the evidence: start with (c)-style jigs and simple B2B tools for known local customers (errors are caught on the bench, compliance is light), use (b) selectively for paid learning, and move to (a) once a niche with repeat demand is visible.
