## 1. Finished-machine resale (cleaning, delivery, AMRs, vendor-certified cobot cells): residual legal duties, vendor terms, prices, adoption

### Takeaway
Reselling a vendor-CE-marked robot keeps the founder out of the manufacturer role as long as they buy from an EU-established entity, sell under the vendor's brand and do not substantially modify the machine; buying directly from a Chinese maker makes the founder the importer, who carries near-manufacturer duties and primary product liability from 9 December 2026. In Czechia the channel already exists with published prices (Pudu CC1 Pro at 379,990 Kč ex VAT, rental from 7,990 Kč per month at Robot World), so a newcomer competes on deployment and support quality rather than on access to the product.

### Cited Findings

Legal roles under the machinery rules
- Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 has separate articles for importers of machinery (Art. 13), importers of partly completed machinery (Art. 14) and distributors (Art. 15). — [Certifico table of contents](https://certifico.com/component/attachments/download/43627)
- A distributor is "any natural or legal person in the supply chain, other than the manufacturer or the importer, who makes a product … available on the market"; distributors must act with due care and verify certain items such as the CE marking. — [DWF](https://dwfgroup.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/7/new-machinery-regulation)
- Importers may only place compliant machinery on the market and must ensure the conformity assessment was carried out and that technical documentation, CE marking and instructions exist. — [DWF](https://dwfgroup.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/7/new-machinery-regulation)
- Importers must mark the machinery with their own name, postal address, website and email, and are the contact for market-surveillance authorities. — reported in the same result set as [Compliance & Risks](https://www.complianceandrisks.com/?p=22688) and [Packmedia](https://packmedia.net/node/10999) (attribution not confirmed)
- Application date: sources in the result set disagree between 14 January 2027 and 20 January 2027; until then the Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC applies. — [DWF](https://dwfgroup.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/7/new-machinery-regulation); [EUR-Lex summary](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/machinery-safety-requirements.html)

Product liability (Directive (EU) 2024/2853)
- Member states must transpose by 9 December 2026; the new rules apply to products placed on the market or put into service after that date. — [Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/resources/product-risk-radar-articles/eu-product-liability-reform); [EUR-Lex summary](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/defective-products-liability.html)
- For products made outside the EU, the importer is in the primary liability chain; liability falls back from manufacturer to importer, then authorised representative, then fulfilment service provider, and finally the distributor. — [Gibson Dunn, March 2026](https://www.gibsondunn.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/eu-product-liability-directive-responding-to-software-ai-and-complex-supply-chains.pdf); [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/eu-introduces-comprehensive-digitalera-product-liability-directive) (attribution within result set not confirmed)
- A distributor becomes liable if no EU-established operator can be identified and it fails, within one month of a request, to name the EU operator or its own supplier. — [Buse](https://buse.de/en/blog-en/commercial-en/what-will-change-with-the-new-eu-product-liability-directive-eu-2024-2853/) (attribution within result set not confirmed)
- Whoever substantially modifies a product outside the original manufacturer's control, including through software updates, configuration changes, self-developed extensions or plugins, is treated as a manufacturer for that modification. — [Xictron](https://www.xictron.com/en/blog/new-product-liability-law-software-december-2026/); [Product Law Perspective, August 2026](https://www.productlawperspective.com/2026/08/eus-new-ai-product-liability-directive-what-manufacturers-need-to-know/)
- Software, including applications, operating systems and AI systems, counts as a product under the directive. — [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2025/01/03/product-liability-directive-in-force-youve-got-just-under-two-years-to-get-r-102jsau)

### Inferences
- Role determines exposure. Buying from Robot World, ASBIS or a vendor's EU entity makes the founder a distributor (verification and traceability duties, fallback liability only). Buying from Shenzhen makes the founder the importer: their name goes on the machine and, from December 2026, an injured person can claim against them as if they were the maker. A solo founder should therefore source only through an EU-established entity and keep the supplier's identity documented for the one-month identification rule.
- The founder's own software is the main way to slide back into manufacturer status. Fleet dashboards, scheduling and reporting that sit beside the robot are low risk; anything that changes the robot's safety behaviour, navigation limits or firmware would count as a modification under both regimes. This matters because software is exactly where this founder would want to add value.
- Mobile cleaning and delivery robots need no fixed installation, so the electrician, safety-consultant and commissioning-engineer dependencies that sank the cell-integrator idea largely fall away. A palletizer or machine-tending cell, even if CE-marked as a finished machine, still gets placed into a customer's line with infeed conveyors and power connection; the workplace risk assessment is the employer's job, but the installer is the one asked when something goes wrong. Cells are closer to the rejected option than the mobile robots are.
- The Czech published prices imply thin room for a sub-reseller. Robot World's CC1 Pro list price (about EUR 15,200) is below the US reseller's ($24,000), and Gausium's advertised 40–60% is for direct distributors. A newcomer under an existing Czech distributor would likely get a much smaller cut; I found no figure for second-tier margins.
- Advantage that accumulates is local: mapped sites, trained customer staff, service response time, and data on which floor types and layouts the robots fail on. None of it is exclusive, and the vendor can appoint more resellers at any time.
- Likely failure modes, from the structure of the market rather than from documented cases: robots returned after a trial because staff do not empty, refill or rescue them; support travel eating the margin on single-unit customers; facility-management companies (OKIN-style) bundling the robot into their own contract; price cuts from Chinese vendors devaluing demo stock.

## 2. Robots-as-a-service operated by the founder

### Takeaway
Published rental rates run at roughly 2–3.5% of the robot's list price per month, which gives the operator a payback of about two and a half to four years before service, financing and idle time; in Czechia the rental offer already exists from the distributor itself at about 8,000 Kč per month for a Pudu cleaning robot. I found no documented small independent RaaS operator in Europe and no hard utilisation data.

### Inferences
- Rent-to-price ratios: Robot World 2.1% per month (CC1 Pro and MT1), RobotLAB 3.4% (CC1 Pro) and 2.8% (Scrubber 75P), Kommersant about 4%. At the Czech ratio a robot bought at list takes about 47 months of continuous rental to gross its price; bought at a 40% distributor discount (about 228,000 Kč) it takes about 29 months. Any idle month, service visit or financing cost lengthens that. A solo operator who buys at reseller-minus prices and rents at the distributor's public rate has little margin.
- Minimum test: one cleaning robot (about EUR 9,000–15,000 depending on discount) placed with one customer on a monthly contract. That is a real proof within weeks and is affordable, but it proves the customer's willingness to pay, not the fleet economics.
- In RaaS the operator, not the customer, owns residual-value risk. Chinese vendors released new models during 2026 (CC1 Pro, KLEENBOT), which pushes down the value of older units held by a small fleet owner.
- Operating the robot at a customer site does not by itself make the founder a manufacturer; it does make them the owner and maintainer, so contractual liability for damage and for downtime needs insurance and clear terms.
- Software leverage is moderate: fleet monitoring, automatic reporting of cleaned area and exception handling can be built by one engineer, and outcome-based pricing (per square metre) rewards that. The binding constraint is still site visits.
- What stops a newcomer: the distributor and facility-management firms already offer rentals; leasing companies will finance a robot for the end customer directly, which removes the reason to rent from a middleman.

### Gaps

## 3. Robot data collection and teleoperation services for AI companies

### Takeaway
Demand is real and funded (Scale AI passed 100,000 production hours by September 2025; egocentric-data startups raised between $5M and $60M rounds in 2026), but prices per hour have fallen by about two-thirds since early 2024 and collection labour is being sourced in India at about $2.50 per hour, so a solo supplier in Czechia has no cost position. The technical debate in 2026 is shifting value from robot teleoperation toward cheap first-person human video, which further favours scale operators.

### Inferences
- Legally this is the lightest variant: no machine is placed on the market, there is no manufacturer role, and the main duties are contracts, data rights and GDPR when filming people or private spaces. One person can start alone.
- Entry capital is trivial (a few hundred euros for SO-101 arms or a UMI gripper, up to about EUR 10,000 for a bimanual rig), and first data can be recorded in days. The hard part is not recording but being accepted as a supplier: buyers measure in thousands of hours, and the suppliers named in coverage are venture-funded aggregators with offshore workforces.
- Unit economics for a solo collector are wage-like. At $118 per packaged hour and falling, one person recording six usable hours a day grosses about $700 a day at best, and only if a buyer takes the whole output; as a sub-contributor to an aggregator the rate is $18–55 per hour in the US and far less elsewhere.
- Where this founder's skills fit is the software layer, not the collection: quality control, automatic episode segmentation and labelling, format conversion to LeRobot-style datasets, and agent-run validation pipelines. That is closer to a tooling or SaaS business sold to data farms than to "work tied to the physical world".
- A defensible small niche would need something the aggregators cannot get cheaply: access to real European workplaces (workshops, kitchens, labs, farms) with consent, or task expertise. I found no evidence that buyers pay a premium for European-site data.
- Durability is the weakest of the five. Prices fell about 65% in two years, the method in favour changed during 2026, and the customer base is a few dozen labs whose own funding is cyclical.
- Typical failure: collecting data on spec that no buyer takes, because format, embodiment or task list does not match what a lab is training on.

### Inferences
- Legal exposure is low: the lab owns a finished CE-marked instrument and the founder supplies Python scripts and labware definitions. The founder carries professional liability for bad protocols (wasted reagents and samples), not machine-manufacturer liability. No licence is required to write protocols.
- This is the most software-heavy variant and the best fit for agent tooling: protocols are Python, a simulator exists, and an agent harness can generate, simulate and regression-test scripts. The same fact is the threat, since the vendor gives away a generator. The paid work that remains is what the vendor excludes: integration with third-party devices, LIMS and scheduling, and validation on real liquids, tips and plates, which needs bench time and wet-lab judgement the founder does not have.
- A realistic entry cost is one OT-2 or Flex (EUR 15,000–25,000 new, less used) to test on, plus consumables. A first paid protocol could come within weeks if a lab already owns an idle robot; finding that lab is the constraint.
- The vendor's price list implies a ceiling of roughly EUR 5,000–14,000 per protocol. With about 430 Czech entities, of which only a fraction own an Opentrons, the local market supports occasional projects, not a pipeline; the customer base would have to be EU-wide and remote.

- Typical failure: the founder can produce code that simulates correctly but fails at the bench (liquid classes, evaporation, tip behaviour), and has no biologist to catch it; or the lab's scientist uses the free AI tool instead.

### Inferences
- One person can do all of it: register as operator, pass the free online test and the A2 theory exam, buy one drone, insure it, and fly. No machine is placed on the market, so no manufacturer liability. Flights over buildings in towns (roofs, facades) are where the rules bite: proximity to uninvolved people pushes work toward A2 limits or the specific category, which needs a declaration or authorisation and more preparation.
- Capital to first paid job is about EUR 7,000–9,000 (drone, batteries, insurance, software), and time to minimum competence is weeks. Thermography has its own competence requirements (irradiance thresholds, emissivity, IEC-style reporting) that customers and insurers ask about; I did not find the Czech expectations.
- The field work cannot be delegated to software, but everything after landing can: stitching, anomaly detection, georeferencing to panel strings, report generation, and tracking degradation across repeat visits. That is where an agent-harness engineer has real leverage over hobbyist pilots, and it is what Dronetech already sells.
- Market size is small. If the roughly 4.4 GW base (year-end 2024) has grown at about 1 GW a year, it would be near 6 GW by late 2026; that extrapolation is mine. Even if 3 GW of ground-mounted and commercial plants were inspected yearly at EUR 300 per MW, the whole Czech market would be under EUR 1M a year. The published small-job prices (2,900–4,000 Kč) barely cover travel.
- Price erosion is visible in the spread: day rates of 25,000 Kč alongside jobs from 990 Kč indicate many part-time operators. Differentiation has to come from the report and repeat monitoring, or from adjacent inspection types (roof moisture, facade heat loss, district-heating leaks) where flying is harder and fewer operators qualify.
- Advantage that accumulates: multi-year defect histories per plant and relationships with O&M companies and insurers. Weak moat, since data stays with the asset owner and platforms sell the analysis as a commodity.
- Typical failure: seasonal income (thermography needs sun), weather cancellations, underpricing by hobbyists, and O&M firms buying their own drone once the workflow is proven.

## 6. Across the variants: how a newcomer gets the first paying customer, how long it takes, and how it usually fails

### Takeaway
Drone inspection and a single rented cleaning robot can reach a paying customer in weeks for under about EUR 15,000 with no outside partner; robot data and lab protocols cost even less to start but depend on being admitted by a small set of remote buyers. ### Inferences
Comparison on the reader's criteria (my assessment from the findings above; time and capital figures are estimates, not sourced):

| Variant | Alone, legally? | Capital and time to first paid proof | Software share | Main blocker | What accumulates |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Resale of finished robots | Yes as distributor via an EU entity; importer role if buying from China | One demo unit, about EUR 9,000–15,000; 1–3 months to a paid pilot | Low to moderate (reports, fleet tools); mostly sales and site work | Existing Czech distributor and thin second-tier margin | Local site know-how, service reputation |
| (b) Founder-operated rental | Yes | Same unit; first rental in weeks if a customer is found | Moderate (monitoring, outcome billing) | Payback of 2.5–4 years at public rental rates; residual-value risk | Fleet data, contracts |
| (c) Robot data services | Yes, lightest regime | Under EUR 1,000 to about EUR 10,000; data in days, contract uncertain | High in tooling, zero in collection | No cost position against India; buyers want scale | Little; demand method is shifting |
| (d) Lab protocol services | Yes | EUR 0–25,000 (own robot optional); weeks if a lab is found | Very high | Vendor's free AI tool and own service; needs wet-lab judgement | Protocol library, niche reputation |
| (e) Drone inspection | Yes in open category; more paperwork over towns | About EUR 7,000–9,000; 2–6 weeks | High after landing, none in the field | Crowded, low prices, small Czech market | Per-asset defect history |

- First-customer routes implied by the findings: (a) and (b) approach a facility manager or retailer with a free one-week trial of one robot, since trials are how Robot World and OKIN sell; (e) offer a discounted first inspection to a commercial rooftop owner or a small O&M firm, using Dronetech's loss figures as the argument; (d) contact core facilities that already own an Opentrons and offer a fixed-price protocol below the vendor's $5,750; (c) apply as a contributor to an aggregator, or pitch tooling to a data farm rather than selling hours.
- The two variants that match "one person, weeks, real customer" best are (e) and (b) with a single unit. Both test demand cheaply; neither shows a path to scale without either hiring field staff or moving the business toward the software layer.
- (a) with vendor-certified cobot cells brings back part of what the reader rejected: site integration, customer line stoppages and questions about who is responsible for the installed whole.
- (c) and (d) are closest to the reader's existing skills but are the least "physical"; in both the physical part is either commodity labour or needs domain expertise the reader lacks.
- A combination is plausible: field service in (e) or (b) as the entry point, with the founder's software (automated reporting, fleet or defect analytics) as the part that could later be sold to other operators. That would be a return to software sold into a physical niche.
