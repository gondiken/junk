### Inferences
- Floor on the Czech billed rate (researcher's arithmetic). Advertised inspector wage 150–180 CZK/h (earlier notes) plus employer social and health contributions of about 34% (background knowledge) gives a direct labour cost of roughly 200–240 CZK/h. Supervision, travel between plants, idle time between jobs, reporting and margin sit on top. A billed rate somewhere around 300–450 CZK/h (about €12–18) would be consistent with that cost base; **this is a derived guess, not a finding**.
- Same exercise for Germany: sorter wages around €14–17/h, statutory minimum wage €13.90 in 2026 (background knowledge), employer on-costs about 21% (background knowledge) give €17–21/h direct cost, so a billed rate in the high €20s to €40s per hour is plausible. Derived guess only.
- Background knowledge, low confidence, unverified: figures the researcher recalls from automotive quality forums put North American third-party sorting at roughly $25–45 per billed hour, with supervisors and engineers billed higher and minimum call-out charges common. No European equivalent recalled with confidence.
- Per-part equivalent (researcher's arithmetic, throughput assumed, not sourced): if a human inspector visually checks 300–1,500 simple parts per hour and is billed at about 350 CZK/h, the customer pays about 0.25–1.2 CZK (€0.01–0.05) per part. A per-part AI service has to price inside that band for simple parts; it only becomes attractive money on slow, complex checks (several faces, gauges, 30–120 parts per hour), where the manual cost is €0.10–0.50 per part.
- Sorting firms' margins are probably thin on labour-only work (the business is close to temporary staffing with a quality wrapper) and better on engineering, residency and rework; unverified.

## 3. How sorting jobs are awarded, how fast, who pays, where the work is done

### Takeaway
The contractual pattern is confirmed in outline: under second-level controlled shipping the customer chooses the third party and the supplier pays. Response times, job lengths and the split between supplier site, customer site and the sorting firm's own premises were not confirmed by any source in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Controlled Shipping Level 1: the supplier's own employees add an inspection step at the supplier's site, contain all suspect material and certify shipments; the customer decides how many shipments need certification (customer-specific supplier-manual clauses collected by a contract database) — [Law Insider, controlled-shipping clauses](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/controlled-shipping)
- Controlled Shipping Level 2: same as Level 1 plus an inspection done by an impartial third party; in the Plasman clause "the third party is selected by Plasman and paid by the supplier" — [Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/controlled-shipping)
- In another customer's clause (AlphaUSA) the scope of Level 2 is set by the customer's quality group and may be a third-party sort, extra certified lots, or paid sorting supervision by the customer's designee — [Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/controlled-shipping)
- Controlled shipping is triggered by recurring non-conformances requiring a recovery plan — [Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/controlled-shipping); related sample clauses on sorting and segregating costs — [Law Insider, sorting and segregating](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/sorting-and-segregating)
- US containment vendors advertise off-site sorting and rework campaigns (parts shipped to the vendor), showing that the ship-to-our-premises model exists; no prices or lead times in extract — [RC Engine Service](https://rcengineservice.com/pages/off-site-sorting)
- Czech academic theses document sorting and repair of non-conforming parts as a standard step in complaint handling at automotive suppliers (chapter titles only; contents not read) — [ZČU thesis on complaints at BOS Automotive Products](https://dspace.zcu.cz/items/fe07f283-cb86-4a25-a022-805136a4c3d3/full); [theses.cz thesis](https://theses.cz/id/pm4aob/zaverecna_prace.pdf?lang=sk)

### Inferences
- Two different buyers exist, and they behave differently. (a) Customer-imposed sorting (CSL2, or a sort at the customer's plant after a line-side defect): the customer or OEM nominates the firm, usually one already approved and physically resident at that plant, and recharges the supplier. A newcomer cannot win this without being on the customer's approved list. (b) Supplier-initiated sorting (CSL1-type containment, firewall inspection before shipping, clearing stock after an internal finding): the supplier's quality manager chooses freely and pays directly. Only (b) is open to a solo newcomer at the start.
- Background knowledge, unverified in this session: containment is expected within 24 hours of a complaint (the 8D interim-containment step, D3), sorting firms advertise call-out within hours to 24 hours, round-the-clock shifts, and resident teams at OEM plants; jobs range from a few days (clear the suspect lot) to months (CSL2 until exit criteria such as a set number of defect-free shipments or days are met). Location is wherever the suspect stock is: customer's line and warehouse first, then supplier's finished-goods store, with firms also running their own halls for longer rework. Stellantis and GM use CSL1/CSL2 language; the Volkswagen group uses its own escalation programme under Formel Q.
- An AI station does not fit the first 24–48 hours of an emergency sort: it needs images of the defect, lighting set-up and validation, while people with a boundary sample start the same day. It fits the long tail: jobs that run for weeks, recurring firewall inspections, and part numbers that return to containment repeatedly.
- Because the customer imposes the method under CSL2, any change from human to camera inspection during an open containment needs the customer's agreement (see question 7).

### Gaps
- Response-time commitments, typical job length, share of work at customer versus supplier versus own premises for Czech and Slovak firms: no source found. Firms' own websites state these and should be read when fetching works.
- OEM-specific rules in Central Europe (Škoda/VW Formel Q escalation, Hyundai/Kia, Stellantis Trnava, Jaguar Land Rover Nitra): not searched.
- Who nominates the sorting firm in supplier-paid sorts at Czech OEM plants (resident-provider lists): not found.

### Inferences
- Route 2 (supplying AI stations to sorting firms) faces a structural problem at the top of the market: the number-one firm has an in-house product and a stated automation strategy. The addressable sorting firms are the regional ones (100% REWORK, Enter-Prise Sorting, STRIX, AQC and similar) and possibly Exact x Forestall and Formel D if they have nothing comparable, which is unknown.
- High-speed bulk parts (screws, washers, rivets, turned parts fed from a vibratory bowl) are already served by dedicated machines running at hundreds to thousands of parts per minute and by contract sorters that own them. At that throughput the price per part is necessarily a tiny fraction of a cent, and the machine has moving parts and feeding hardware. This is not a market for a camera-on-a-stand station; avoid it.
- The open space is the part that today is sorted by hand because it is too large, too varied or too low-volume for a sorting machine: moulded plastic parts, stampings, castings, assemblies, trim. Here an operator still has to present the part, so the AI station replaces judgement, not handling. Labour saving per station is therefore partial (faster and more consistent decisions, one person instead of two, less re-inspection), not full.
- No per-part or per-job rental offer was found from anyone. That is weak evidence either way: 14 searches is too few to call the niche empty.
- Background knowledge, unverified: TRIGO bought Scortex (a Paris startup founded about 2016) around 2022; German contract sorters with their own automatic machines ("Lohnsortierung") exist for fasteners and turned parts and typically quote per 1,000 pieces.

## 7. Regulatory and contractual obstacles to operating an inspection service

### Takeaway
No dedicated search was possible, so nothing here is sourced beyond the controlled-shipping clauses. From background knowledge the main obstacle is not a licence but acceptance: in automotive the inspection method belongs to the control plan and the customer's containment instruction, so a camera replacing a human sorter needs the supplier's and often the customer's sign-off, backed by an attribute measurement-system study.

### Cited Findings
- Under Level 2 controlled shipping the customer selects the third party and defines the scope, which means the customer also controls what counts as an acceptable inspection — [Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/controlled-shipping)

### Inferences
All points below are background knowledge, not confirmed in this session; verify before relying on them.
- Method approval. IATF 16949 requires measurement-system analysis for inspection equipment named in the control plan, and has specific requirements for appearance items (lighting, masters, competent evaluators). For a pass/fail check the usual evidence is an attribute agreement study in the AIAG MSA manual style: about 50 parts including borderline and defective ones, repeated trials, agreement and miss-rate and false-alarm criteria. In German supply chains VDA Volume 5 plays the same role. An AI station can pass such a study, and a documented study is a selling point, because human visual sorters are rarely held to the same test.
- Customer consent. For supplier-initiated firewall inspection the supplier's quality manager can accept the method. For customer-imposed containment, changing the method needs the customer's supplier-quality engineer to agree; expect to run camera and human in parallel first.
- Accreditation. ISO/IEC 17020 accreditation for inspection bodies exists but is not normally demanded for sorting; sorting firms generally hold ISO 9001. A one-person firm without ISO 9001 will be asked about it by automotive buyers.
- Liability. Sorting contracts typically promise effort, not a zero-defect outcome, and cap liability at the job fee or an insured sum; customers push for recharge of any escape after sorting. A solo operator needs a written cap, professional/product liability insurance, and should avoid being the sole release gate at first (consistent with the earlier notes).
- Czech trade licence. Sorting and inspection services fall under the unregulated "free trade" (volná živnost), most likely the listed activity "Testování, měření, analýzy a kontroly"; no professional qualification is required. Supplying workers who work under the customer's direction would be agency employment and needs a labour-office permit, so the offer must be a service with its own method and responsibility, not hired hands.
- Product law. Operating one's own station as a service means the station is never placed on the market, which removes the CE declaration burden described in the earlier notes for sold stations (general workplace-safety and electrical-safety duties for own equipment remain). Renting a station to a customer does count as making it available and brings the CE duties back.

**Implications for the three routes (researcher's judgement; evidence is thin and mostly negative)**
- Route 1, sell or rent stations to small manufacturers: nothing in this round changes the earlier picture. The missing fact that matters most is the buyer's comparison price (the sorting invoice), which is still unknown.
- Route 2, supply sorting firms: weakened by the finding that TRIGO owns Scortex Spark and is growing it fast. Still testable with regional firms, whose incentive is mixed (a station cuts billable hours unless they bill per part or are short of staff). One conversation with an owner of a 50–300-person Czech sorting firm would settle more than further desk research.
- Route 3, operate a service: the only fully open door is supplier-initiated sorting at the supplier's site or with parts shipped to the founder's premises; customer-nominated containment is closed to an unlisted newcomer. The founder would be competing with labour billed at perhaps €12–18 per hour in Czechia, so a per-part price has to undercut roughly €0.01–0.05 per simple part while a person still handles the parts. The service makes economic sense mainly on long-running or recurring sorts and on slow, judgement-heavy checks, and as a way to get paid while collecting defect images that later sell a station (route 1).
- Fastest evidence, in order: (1) obtain two or three real sorting quotes or invoices from tier-2 suppliers to fix the hourly rate; (2) pull filed accounts of five Czech sorting firms for revenue per head; (3) ask two regional sorting firms whether they would trial a station on a long job, and on what commercial terms.

- Liability terms in Czech sorting firms' general terms and conditions; insurance cost for a solo inspection service: not searched.
- Exact name and number of the free-trade activity and whether any Czech rule treats on-site sorting as disguised agency employment: not verified.
